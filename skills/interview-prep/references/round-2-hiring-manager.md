# Round 2: the hiring manager

**What this round is.** The first round where someone who will live with the decision is
listening. The hiring manager is answering one question: *can this person do the job on my team,
starting soon?* Everything else is in service of that.

It has four parts, and they are usually run in this order:

1. **Experience.** Depth on what the resume claims. Not "what did you do" but "how, and why that
   way, and what did it cost".
2. **The role, in depth.** The hiring manager will describe the job properly for the first time.
   This is a two-way conversation and the user is expected to have questions that could only come
   from having listened in round 1.
3. **Technical questions, generalized to the role.** Not a coding test. Judgment questions:
   architecture choices, trade-offs, how they would approach a problem the team actually has.
4. **Management scenarios.** Success and challenge. This is usually the part that decides it, and
   the part candidates prepare least.

**Round 1 feeds this directly.** The manager's name, the real team size, the problem the recruiter
named, the band: all of it was collected in the screen and all of it changes this file. Open with
`## What the last round established`, and cite `1-screen-debrief.md` rather than reasoning from
the posting where the two disagree.

## Sections to write

### What the last round established

Four things, from the debrief:

- What the recruiter said about the role that the posting did not.
- What was learned about this manager specifically.
- What the recruiter said this round would cover. If they said it, prep that, and prep it first.
- **What the user answered badly in round 1.** It may well be asked again, by someone who read
  the recruiter's notes. Write the better version here.

### Experience: three stories, not a tour

Pick **three** accomplishments, each mapped to one of the top must-haves in `analysis.md`, each
traceable to atoms. Three is the number: one is thin, five will not fit, and a manager who asks a
follow-up wants depth rather than the next headline.

For each story write:

- **The one-line claim** — what it is, in the employer's vocabulary from the analysis table.
- **The 90-second version**, written to be spoken: the situation, the decision the user made, what
  the team did, what changed, with the atom's numbers unaltered.
- **The follow-ups it invites, and the answers.** This is the part that matters. A good story gets
  probed: what went wrong, what you would do differently, who disagreed, how you knew it worked,
  what it cost. Write two or three, honestly. A story with no cost attached reads as marketing.
- **The atom IDs**, so the claim is checkable against the profile.

Reuse `profile/interview-answers.md` where an answer already exists. If a story is retold here in
a form that would work at any company, that is a signal it belongs in the answer bank; note it and
let `interview-debrief` promote it after the round.

### The role, in depth

The manager will describe the job. The user's job is to have questions that prove they were
listening in round 1 and have thought since. Write the specific ones, drawn from the screen:

- What the recruiter said the hard problem is, put back to the manager for their version. The two
  answers rarely match, and the difference is the most useful thing in the round.
- The shape of the team: who is on it, what seniority, what is missing, what the open role is
  actually for. Backfill and growth are different jobs.
- What the first ninety days look like, in their words rather than a generic answer.
- What this manager expects from a manager reporting to them, or beside them: how much autonomy,
  what they want to hear about, what they do not.

### Technical questions, generalized

Not algorithms. Derive three to five from the analysis's must-haves and inferred priorities, in
the domain the team actually works in, and answer them in the file:

- Architecture and trade-off questions on the systems named in the posting.
- "How would you approach X" where X is the problem the posting implies.
- Depth checks on the specific technologies the resume claims.
- **The gaps from `match.md`.** Every one of them gets a written boundary sentence: the honest
  limit, said before it is discovered, followed by the true adjacent evidence. The line that works
  is "fluent enough to review the work and reason about the design, honest about where the
  specialists start". The line that fails is claiming depth that a follow-up will find.

For a management role, add the question they always ask and few candidates answer well: **how
hands-on are you, actually.** Write the version with a date on it, from atoms, and state its
boundary in the same breath.

### Management scenarios

Ask for a success and a challenge, and be ready for these categories. Write **two prepared
scenarios** in full and a one-line pointer for the rest, because five fully-written scenarios will
not be read.

| Category | The question behind it |
|---|---|
| Underperformer | Will you act, or will you let it rot and call it kindness? |
| Conflict in the team, or with a peer team | Do you resolve it or escalate it? |
| Delivery slipping | Do you tell people early? What did you cut? |
| Attrition, a regretted departure | Did you know it was coming? What changed after? |
| Reorg or a priority reversal | Can you carry a decision you disagreed with? |
| Hiring, and a hire that did not work | Do you own the miss? |
| Growing someone, or a promotion denied | Do you develop people or only deploy them? |
| Disagreeing with your own manager | Do you have a spine and a professional register at once? |

Shape each written scenario as: the situation in two sentences, **the decision the user made and
why**, what happened, and what they changed afterwards. The last part is what separates an answer
from an anecdote. Use "I" for the decision and "the team" for the execution.

The challenge scenario must contain a real cost. A "challenge" where everything worked out is
heard as a candidate who will not tell their manager bad news.

### Questions to ask them

Priority ordered. The ones that only a hiring manager can answer:

1. What does success look like for this person at six months, concretely.
2. What is the hardest thing about this team right now.
3. How is the team performing against what this manager wants, and where is the gap.
4. What happened to the last person in this role, or why the headcount exists.
5. How decisions get made between this team and the ones around it.
6. What this manager is like to work for, asked directly.

## What to collect for round 3

Round 3 is the round this one makes possible. The checklist:

- **The exact format of round 3**: who is in it, how long, whether the system design is
  whiteboard, shared editor or discussion, whether there is a take-home.
- **The domain the system design will sit in.** Managers usually answer this if asked plainly,
  and it converts an unbounded exercise into a bounded one.
- **Which claims the manager probed.** Whatever they pushed on will be pushed on harder by the
  panel, with more specialist knowledge behind it.
- **The values or behaviours the manager named**, in their words, for the team-fit section.
- **Anything the manager said they were unsure about.** That is round 3's real agenda.
- Names and titles of the panel, so the user can find out what each person actually cares about.
